# South African single-private-hospital portal: evidence and pilot constraints

Verified through live official-source searches/retrieval on **15 November 2026**. This is product/review research, not a legal opinion or certification. Scope: one hospital, with patient records, documents/signing, concerns, clinical coordination, appointments/reminders, medical-scheme administration, external records, video, laboratory/discharge information and assistants.

The supplied Next/Prisma/SQLite application is a **synthetic-data demonstration with simulated providers**. These sources establish obligations and design constraints; they do not establish that this implementation meets them. A real hospital must approve the intended workflows, document catalogue, clinical governance, contracts and production controls before identifiable patient data is introduced.

## Primary sources and their implications

| # | Official source; date and applicability | Verified rule or guidance; implication |

| 1 | [Protection of Personal Information Act 4 of 2013, Department of Justice-hosted text](https://www.justice.gov.za/legislation/acts/2013-004.pdf). Gazette 26 November 2013; principal processing provisions commenced 1 July 2020; transitional compliance period ended 1 July 2021. Retrieved copy includes historical commencement detail. **Law.** | Health information is special personal information (ss 26–32). Section 32 permits necessary treatment/care and institutional/practice administration; consent is not the sole possible basis, and ordinary processing conditions still apply. Map hospital, practitioners, laboratory, schemes and vendor roles by actual purpose. Sections 19–21 require reasonable technical/organisational security and a written operator contract covering those measures; operators must immediately notify the responsible party of suspected unauthorised access/acquisition. Sections 18, 23–24 address notices and access/correction. Section 72 requires a lawful basis for foreign transfers, including adequate protection or another listed ground; it does **not** impose blanket South African hosting. Section 57 can trigger prior authorisation for specified identifier-linking or inadequately protected foreign transfers of special/children's information. Section 71 constrains solely automated decisions with legal/substantial effects. |
| 2 | [Information Regulator: handling security compromises, fact sheet](https://inforegulator.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Fact-Sheet-on-security-compromises-2.pdf). Published by the Regulator in August 2025. **Regulator guidance implementing POPIA s 22.** | Responsible parties report to the Regulator through eServices and notify affected subjects; an operator reports immediately to its responsible party. The Regulator says low-risk compromises are reportable, and investigation need not be complete before reporting. POPIA's statutory timing is as soon as reasonably possible, not a universal 72-hour rule. **Recommendation:** prepare an incident playbook with named hospital/vendor contacts, evidence preservation and notification ownership before a live pilot. |
| 3 | [National Health Act 61 of 2003, NDoH-hosted Gazette](https://www.health.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/NHREC-NHA-of-2003.pdf). Published 23 July 2004; core patient-rights/records provisions operative. This is the original Gazette, not a fully current consolidation. **Law: relevant ss 6–8, 10, 13–18.** | Provide understandable information and obtain informed consent to the specified health service, subject to statutory exceptions. The person in charge must ensure records are created and maintained. Patient information is confidential; s 14 disclosure routes include written consent and specified legal/public-health exceptions, while s 15 permits necessary legitimate care disclosures in the patient's interests. Section 17 requires controls against unauthorised record access, copying, alteration and destruction. Patients have complaint rights under s 18. **Inference:** neither employment at the hospital nor a generic portal consent grants unrestricted records access; past-doctor retrieval needs a verified identity, lawful disclosure route and authentic source. |
| 4 | [Norms and standards regulations applicable to different categories of health establishments, GN 67](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201802/41419gon67.pdf). Gazette 41419, 2 February 2018. **Regulations**; scope is tied to applicable measurement tools (reg 2), so hospital/OHSC applicability must be checked locally. | Reg 6 requires protected confidential records, a maintained record system, biographical/contact and clinical-intervention information, a formal informed-consent process and discharge reports. Reg 4 addresses service/fee information and complaints/compliments/suggestions systems; reg 5 addresses triage and referrals, including retaining referral documents. **Recommendation:** concerns need assigned staff, acknowledgement, escalation and closure; critical laboratory results, referrals and discharge handovers need an accountable clinical owner. An unmonitored inbox cannot establish those processes. |
| 5 | [HPCSA Booklet 9: keeping patient records](https://www.hpcsa-blogs.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Booklet-9-Keeping-of-Patient-Records_Review-Draft_-FINAL_Sept-2022.pdf). Revised **September 2022**; Council Corporate Affairs published it in November 2022 and its June 2025 index still points to that edition. The filename contains “Review-Draft”; Council distribution and document cover identify the revision. **Professional ethical guidance.** | Sections 3–5 cover attribution, dates, corrections and care communications, including telephone/virtual encounters. Section 7 recommends indefinite retention where practical; otherwise generally at least six years from last treatment/dormancy. Exceptions include minors until at least 21, mentally incapacitated patients for life, occupational records 20 years and recommended retention of at least 25 years for delayed-manifestation risk; statutory requirements may override. Billing and clinical records have different access purposes. **Recommendation:** define record categories, legal holds and attributable amendments; do not automatically erase the complete record six years after upload or on consent withdrawal. |
| 6 | [HPCSA Booklet 10: telehealth](https://www.hpcsa-blogs.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Booklet-10_Telehealth__Dec_2021_.pdf). Revised **December 2021**; still indexed by the Council in June 2025. **Professional ethical guidance**, not approval of a video platform. | Sections 3–5 require clinically appropriate use, practitioner responsibility, documented evaluation/history and records; an online questionnaire alone is inadequate. An established relationship is desirable rather than universally compulsory; the booklet says practitioners must not offer an exclusively telehealth practice. Section 5.7 requires informed consent to treatment/technology, written or orally recorded, and addresses identity/location, confidentiality risks, third parties and costs. Section 5.8 includes electronic communications, prescriptions, laboratory/past-care information and consents in the care record. **Recommendation:** add suitability assessment, consent capture, clinician identity, in-person/emergency escalation and documented encounter closure around video. |
| 7 | [HPCSA Booklet 20: ethical use of artificial intelligence](https://www.hpcsa-blogs.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/ETHICAL-GUIDELINES-USE-OF-AI.pdf). **September 2025**, published on Council Corporate Affairs site November 2025. **Professional ethical guidance.** | Sections 3.5 and 9 keep final clinical decisions and accountability with the practitioner. Section 6.2 addresses informing patients when AI assists diagnosis/treatment and protecting care access if they refuse. Section 4.6 points medical-device AI to SAHPRA requirements; s 2.2 says HPCSA does not approve technologies/devices. **Recommendation:** first assistants draft clerical material or retrieve permitted information; staff verify outputs and authorise consequential actions. Log inputs/sources, model/output version, reviewer and disposition without exposing excess health data to vendors. Do not market an autonomous clinician or “HPCSA-approved AI.” |
| 8 | [Electronic Communications and Transactions Act 25 of 2002, official Gazette](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/201409/a25-02.pdf). Published 2 August 2002. **Law**; ss 12–16 and 18 supply the relevant electronic-record/signature framework. Original Gazette, not full amended consolidation. | An electronic signature has legal effect subject to the Act's conditions. Under s 13(1), where law requires a signature without specifying its type, a data-message requirement is met only by an **advanced electronic signature**, defined through an accredited process. Where parties require a signature but have not agreed its type, s 13(3) uses identification, approval and appropriate reliability. Sections 12, 14–16 address accessible writing, integrity/originals, evidence and retention; s 18 addresses notarised/certified documents. **Recommendation:** classify each hospital document and applicable law/professional requirement. Preserve exact version, signer/authority, intent, timestamp and integrity evidence. A drawn signature, typed name, OTP or general consent checkbox is not automatically sufficient for every document, prescription or certificate. |
| 9 | [Medical Schemes Act General Regulations, CMS-hosted text](https://www.medicalschemes.com/files/Acts%20and%20Regulations/GNR1262%20of%2020%20October%201999.pdf). GN R 1262, 20 October 1999; this downloadable consolidation lists amendments through 3 December 2004. **Law**, with historical-copy limitation: verify later amendments and contracted scheme rules before encoding production logic. Relevant PMB principles are also described in current CMS guidance below. | Reg 5 specifies claim/account details, including member/patient identifiers, provider/practice identifiers, diagnostic/item codes, dates and costs. Reg 6 protects submission/resubmission time and requires notice of erroneous/unacceptable claims. Reg 8 permits managed-care/preauthorisation mechanisms subject to its protections; reg 8(6) prohibits making appropriate emergency intervention wait for authorisation. Reg 8's full PMB-payment rule has DSP and involuntary-use qualifications. **Recommendation:** treat emergency care, authorisation, benefit eligibility, claim submission, adjudication and payment as distinct states; keep original scheme messages, references, scope, expiry, rejection reasons and appeals. |
| 10 | [Council for Medical Schemes: prescribed minimum benefits](https://www.medicalschemes.co.za/resources/pmb/). Live regulator consumer guidance checked 15 November 2026; page has no verified publication date. Indexed content retrieved; direct full-page fetch timed out. **Guidance**, not substitute scheme-specific contract/rules. | PMBs provide minimum benefits regardless of selected option, subject to governing rules. CMS explains DSP use, emergency access, correct ICD-10 information and support for PMB claims. Its consumer guidance advises following up on submission within four months and payment within 30 days of receipt. **Recommendation:** use deadline tracking with the exact applicable statutory/scheme rule, receipt acknowledgements and correction/resubmission events; avoid reducing the rule to a guessed 120-day timer. A local portal “approved” status cannot establish real scheme authorisation or guarantee payment. No universal scheme API, access entitlement or commercial integration agreement was verified here. |
| 11 | [2021 Health Normative Standards Framework for interoperability in digital health, NDoH Gazette](https://www.health.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/HNSF_Gazette_21_October_2022.pdf). GN 2667, Gazette 47337, **21 October 2022**; explicitly supersedes 2014 HNSF and includes private-sector participation. **Gazetted framework under NHA s 74(1).** | Scope covers exchange between systems and shared national health infrastructure/EHR; it expressly does not prescribe internal database structure. Uses IHE profiles, with HL7/CDA/FHIR and other underlying standards according to the use case. FHIR versions follow the selected profile; the framework avoids imposing one universal FHIR version. **Recommendation:** agree partner-specific identity matching, terminology, provenance, document/result formats, acknowledgements and reconciliation. A FHIR-shaped JSON payload alone proves neither interoperability nor conformance; laboratory, hospital/PAS, doctor and scheme interfaces require actual counterpart agreements and testing. |
| 12 | [SAHPRA guideline for classification of medical devices and IVDs, v5](https://www.sahpra.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/SAHPGL-MD-04_v5-Guideline-for-Classification-of-MD-and-IVDs.pdf). **27 February 2025**, effective February 2025; official guideline. **Regulatory guidance** applying Medicines Act/device regulations, not blanket product approval. | Sections 3.1–3.2 base classification on intended use and risk; software meeting the medical-device definition is an active device. Software controlling/influencing a device generally follows its classification; accessories are classified separately. Intended purpose is evidenced by labelling, instructions, advertising and technical documentation. SAHPRA can resolve uncertain classification. **Inference:** record storage, scheduling and administrative drafting do not automatically make the entire portal a medical device. Diagnostic interpretation, treatment recommendation, clinical risk scoring or device-control features require a documented intended-use assessment and specialist/SAHPRA determination where uncertain. No approval or class is established for this demonstration. |

## Recommended evidence before a live single-hospital pilot

These are proposed acceptance artefacts, not claims that every item is individually mandated by one statute:

1. **Hospital ownership:** named executive sponsor, clinical safety lead, Information Officer, record custodian, document approvers and each operational queue owner; a signed pilot scope and downtime/escalation plan.
2. **Data and suppliers:** data-flow/purpose register, lawful-basis decisions, retention/access schedule, operator/subprocessor contracts, foreign-transfer assessment and vendor controls for messaging, video, storage and AI. List providers as simulated until real credentials, contracts and successful transactions exist.
3. **Access and identity:** verified staff/patient/proxy identities; least-privilege, patient/care-relationship restrictions; privileged-access review, audit evidence and an accountable emergency-access process. Role names alone do not establish suitable access control.
4. **Reliable workflows:** source-of-truth decisions and reconciliation for hospital records, bookings, laboratory results, discharge, past-doctor records and scheme messages. Validate duplicate/wrong-patient handling, critical-result escalation, failed reminders and duplicate authorisation submissions.
5. **Production assurance:** threat model, independent security assessment and resolved material findings; backup restoration and downtime drills; incident exercise; clinician-led workflow validation and staff training. Agree measurable success/stop criteria and a limited real-user cohort.
6. **Reviewable commercial scope:** provider quotations, hospital support obligations, operating costs, licensing responsibilities, deployment/support arrangements and liability allocation. This research establishes no budget, certification, procurement success, regulatory sign-off or production-readiness conclusion.

Current applicability is limited to the provisions and editions identified above. Before rollout, the hospital's legal/compliance and clinical teams should validate the final document catalogue and any intervening amendments, provincial licence conditions, OHSC measurement tools and partner contracts. The sources support a carefully governed pilot path; they do not support describing the current demo as commercially or legally ready.

## Additional 2026 claims-processing update

[Information Regulator Notice 7198, Gazette 54268, 6 March 2026](https://www.gov.za/sites/default/files/gcis_document/202603/54268gon7198.pdf) regulates health-information processing by its listed responsible parties and applicable operators, including medical schemes, administrators and managed-care organisations. Check the listed scope against each claims/cloud/operator relationship; it is not a blanket statement that every hospital activity falls within those regulations. This update belongs in the hospital information officer's pre-pilot review alongside POPIA and the actual provider contracts. The source and scope were checked against the official Gazette.
